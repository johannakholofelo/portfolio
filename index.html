Quantitative & Analytical Showcase

Quantitative economic analysis is important because it allows researchers to investigate economic patterns using measurable evidence.


Artifact 1: Econometric Analysis of South African Sectoral Employment Dynamics.


Raw Dataset Source: Statistics South Africa (Stats SA) Quarterly Labour Force Survey (QLFS), Q4:2024

Methodology: This project employed an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Multiple Linear Regression model to analyze the structural determinants of employment across various sectors in South Africa. The dependent variable was formal employment status, modeled against independent variables including educational attainment level, geographic location (urban vs. rural), age, and industry classification. Multi-collinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), and White’s test was applied to control for heteroscedasticity.


Artifact 2: Inflation and the cost of living in South Africa.


Raw Dataset Source: South African Reserve Bank (SARB) Quarterly Bulletin (Time-Series Data, 2014–2024)

Methodology: This study utilized an Autoregressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) cointegration approach to examine the long-run relationship between money supply growth (M3), the repo rate, and consumer priceindex (CPI) inflation in South Africa. Before estimation, the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test was used to confirm that the variables were a mixof \(I(0)\) and \(I(1)\) integration levels. An Error Correction Model (ECM)was subsequently specified to determine the speed of adjustment back tolong-run equilibrium following a monetary shock

Policy Analysis and Research

Evaluating Labour Market Policies to Address Unemployment in South Africa Unemployment is a structural and macroeconomic challenge that affects income, poverty, productivity and social inclusion. A policy response therefore needs to address both the supply of labour and the demand for workers. This policy analysis considers skills development, small-business support, productive infrastructure investment and employment-matching services. Human capital theory suggests that relevant education, training and experience can improve productivity and employability.


Keynesian analysis highlights the role of aggregate demand and productive public expenditure, while labour-market theory draws attention to matching frictions and barriers to employment. Policy recommendations include strengthening vocational education and workplace learning; supporting viable small and medium-sized enterprises through finance and market access; prioritising productive infrastructure and maintenance; and improving job-search, placement and career-guidance services. The policies should be evaluated using employment creation, job retention, earnings, participation, programme costs and the sustainability of outcomes. Skills training alone cannot solve unemployment if the economy does not generate sufficient vacancies. Similarly, public employment can provide short-term relief but should be distinguished from sustainable private-sector employment.

Critical Reflection: Artifact 1


An important limitation concerns the quality, frequency, and comparability of the data. GDP growth and unemployment may be measured differently across datasets, and changes in survey methods or statistical definitions can affect comparisons over time.

Annual observations also conceal short-term movements in the economy. Furthermore, unemployment is influenced by factors beyond economic growth, including education, skills mismatches, investment, technological change, labour market institutions, and the structure of production

Artifact 2: Inflation and the cost of living in South Africa.


This project will improve my ability to interpret price indices and distinguish between a change in the price level and a change in the inflation rate. It will also help me understand that a reduction in inflation does not necessarily mean that prices have returned to their previous levels; it means that prices are increasing more slowly. After analysing the data, I will compare the observed trends across expenditure categories and consider their implications for household welfare. Overall, this project will strengthen my quantitative skills and encourage me to evaluate economic indicators critically rather than treating statistical measures as complete representations of economic reality.

Reflection summary across the four artifacts


Artifact 1: Economic growth and unemployment Research approach: Correlation and regression This project focuses on the relationship between GDP growth and unemployment. The main lesson is that statistical relationships require careful interpretation. Economic growth may be associated with changes in unemployment, but other influences must be considered before making causal claims.


Artifact 2: Inflation and the cost of living Research approach: CPI calculations and trend analysis This project examines changes in consumer prices and household purchasing power. The key lesson is that national inflation figures may not reflect every household's experience because consumption patterns and the importance of essential goods differ.


Artifact 3: Labour market policy Research approach: Theoretical and policy evaluation This project compares skills development, small business support, public investment, and employment services. The central lesson is that policy effectiveness depends on implementation, labour demand, funding, and the sustainability of employment outcomes.
